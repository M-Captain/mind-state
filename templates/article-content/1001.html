Just One More Scroll

It usually starts innocently.

You get into bed at 11:30. You tell yourself you’ll sleep in five minutes. So naturally, you open Instagram.

One reel. Then another.

Then somehow, you are watching a 47-part story about a woman who quit her job, moved to Italy, and makes pottery for a living. You don’t even like pottery.

You check the time.

1:17 a.m.

You panic for approximately three seconds before deciding that, since you’ve already stayed up this late, you might as well keep scrolling.

And that’s how tomorrow’s tiredness is created.

We often think staying up late is just about losing a few hours of sleep. But it slowly starts affecting much more than the number of hours we spend in bed. Our mornings become harder, our attention gets worse, our patience becomes suspiciously low, and suddenly the smallest inconvenience feels like a personal attack.

The problem isn’t necessarily the phone itself. It’s the combination of endless scrolling and delayed sleep. There is always another video, another notification, another message, another piece of information waiting for us. There is no natural ending point. A book eventually has a last page. A television episode eventually ends. Instagram seems to have personally decided that you should never experience closure.

And the more tired we get, the harder it becomes to stop.

Research has consistently connected nighttime screen use with poorer sleep, particularly among children and adolescents. A National Sleep Foundation expert panel found that screen use and the content consumed before sleep can interfere with sleep health in children and teenagers.

There is also the obvious problem: you are choosing to stay awake.

If you scroll until 2 a.m. and have to wake up at 7, your body isn’t going to accept the explanation that “but I was watching something really interesting.”

Teenagers generally need around 8–10 hours of sleep, according to the CDC. Most of us know this. We just seem to treat sleep recommendations like terms and conditions, something we technically agree to but never actually read.

The effects show up the next day.

You wake up tired. You press snooze five times. You walk into class and spend the first ten minutes trying to remember why you’re there. You read the same sentence three times. You have work to finish, but your brain suddenly considers staring at the wall a perfectly reasonable alternative.

Sleep matters for concentration, mood, and how well we function during the day. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute even describes sleep as something people often sacrifice when life gets busy, despite the fact that enough sleep can support being happier and more productive during the day.

And then there is the mood.

When you’re exhausted, everything feels slightly more dramatic. A small disagreement becomes a huge argument. A bad grade feels like the end of your academic career. Someone leaving you on “seen” suddenly becomes a philosophical crisis.

Of course, one late night isn’t going to ruin your life. Sometimes you genuinely have an assignment, an exam, a late conversation, or simply a bad night. The problem is when “I’ll sleep early tomorrow” becomes a daily promise that tomorrow never seems to keep.

There is another interesting part of this habit: sometimes we don’t even scroll because we’re entertained. We scroll because we don’t want the day to end.

During the day, there are classes, work, expectations, conversations, and responsibilities. At night, nobody is asking anything from you. It’s quiet. Your phone becomes a tiny doorway into a world where you don’t have to think about tomorrow yet.

That can make staying awake feel like stealing time back for yourself.

But eventually, you’re not really getting extra time. You’re borrowing it from tomorrow.

And tomorrow collects the debt.

You don’t have to completely delete every social media app, throw your phone into a river, and become a monk. The solution can be much less dramatic. Putting the phone away a little earlier, turning off unnecessary notifications, having a consistent bedtime, or simply deciding that “one more reel” is actually the last reel can make a difference. The NIH recommends using the period before bed for quieter activities and limiting bright artificial light.

Maybe the real challenge is learning that not every quiet moment needs to be filled.

Sometimes you can just lie there. Think.

Because the next morning, your future self is going to have to deal with whatever your 1 a.m. self decided was more important than going to bed.

And honestly, your future self deserves better than that.
