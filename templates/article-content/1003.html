You Don’t Have to Love Your Body Every Day

There are days when you look in the mirror and think, “Wow. I look good.”

And then there are days when you look in the mirror, immediately change your outfit three times, and decide that mirrors are probably unnecessary inventions.

We’ve been told that the solution is body positivity. Love your body. Celebrate every part of it. Look in the mirror and remind yourself that you are beautiful.

It’s a nice idea. But what if you don’t feel beautiful that day?

What if you’re bloated? What if your skin is breaking out? What if your clothes don’t fit the way you wanted them to? What if you’re just having one of those days when you don’t particularly like what you see?

Trying to convince yourself that you love your body can sometimes feel like another thing you’re failing at.

This is where body neutrality comes in. Instead of asking you to love your body, body neutrality asks you to stop making your body responsible for your happiness.

Your body doesn’t have to be beautiful every day. It doesn’t have to be your favourite thing about yourself. It doesn’t even have to be something you think about that much.

Your body is simply… your body.

It gets you to class. It lets you hug your friends. It carries your groceries. It dances badly at parties. It gets tired. It gets sick. It changes. It grows. It sometimes makes weird noises at extremely inappropriate moments.

And perhaps that is enough.

The idea of body neutrality is partly about shifting the focus from how your body looks to what your body does and how you treat it. Instead of standing in front of a mirror trying to convince yourself that every part of you is beautiful, you might simply think, “This is my body today. I don’t need to judge it.”

That sounds much easier.

Because realistically, nobody is going to wake up every morning overflowing with unconditional love for their stomach, thighs, nose, hair, skin, and every other body part.

Some mornings you feel great. Some mornings you don’t. And neither feeling needs to become a verdict about your worth.

Social media makes this particularly difficult. We are constantly looking at carefully chosen photographs of people who appear to have perfect skin, perfect bodies, perfect hair, and somehow perfect lighting at all times. Meanwhile, we’re looking at ourselves under the fluorescent light in a bathroom that was clearly designed by someone who hates human beings.

It is very easy to start comparing. But body neutrality offers another way of thinking: maybe you don’t need to win the comparison. Maybe you can simply stop playing.

You can exercise because it makes you feel stronger, not because you need to punish yourself for eating dessert.

You can eat vegetables because they make you feel good, while also eating the pizza because, well, pizza.

You can wear clothes that make you comfortable instead of constantly asking whether they make you look thinner.

And you can have a bad body-image day without turning it into a bad-self-worth day.

There is also something freeing about accepting that your body will change.

Weight changes. Skin changes. Hair changes. Bodies age. Hormones do their own mysterious little experiments. You cannot realistically spend your entire life trying to keep your body exactly the same.

So maybe the goal isn’t to love every version of yourself.

Maybe it’s to make peace with the fact that there will be many versions of yourself.

Body positivity says, “I love my body.”

Body neutrality says, “My body is not the most interesting thing about me.”

And honestly, that can be a relief. Because you have other things to worry about.

Your friendships. Your work. Your ambitions. The book you’re reading. The person you have a crush on. Why you opened your phone five minutes ago and have already forgotten what you were supposed to do.

Your body can simply exist in the background while you get on with your life. You don’t have to love your reflection every day.

You just have to remember that you are more than a reflection.
