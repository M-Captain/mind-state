A Little Comfort on a Bad Day

There are some days when cooking feels like an unreasonable amount of effort.

You could make a salad. You could eat something healthy. You could prepare an actual meal with vegetables and protein and all the things you’re supposed to eat.

Instead, you are standing in front of the fridge at 9 p.m., emotionally exhausted, holding a tub of ice cream like it contains the answers to your problems.

And honestly, sometimes it helps.

There is something strangely comforting about food on a bad day. Maybe it’s a bowl of creamy mac and cheese, a grilled cheese sandwich with tomato soup, a plate of crispy fries, or a giant bowl of ramen that is probably much too hot, but you eat it anyway.

Comfort food is not really about being hungry.

Sometimes, it’s about wanting something familiar.

Food can be tied to memories, routines, and people. A particular soup might remind you of being sick as a child and having someone look after you. Pancakes on a Sunday morning might remind you of home. Chocolate chip cookies might remind you of baking with friends. Even something as simple as a bowl of warm pasta can feel reassuring when everything else feels slightly out of control.

And that’s probably why comfort food exists in almost every culture.

In the United States, it might be mac and cheese, mashed potatoes, grilled cheese, or chicken noodle soup. In Italy, a bowl of warm pasta can be the ultimate uncomplicated meal. In Japan, ramen and okayu, a soft rice porridge, are often associated with warmth and comfort. In France, there is something deeply reassuring about a bowl of onion soup or a warm gratin.

Different countries, different foods, same basic idea:

“Today was awful. Give me something warm.”

There is actually some science behind why certain foods can feel comforting. Food is connected to memory, emotion, and reward, and eating something we enjoy can activate the brain’s reward systems. But comfort food isn’t a magical cure for sadness. Eating a tub of ice cream will not solve your exam results, fix your friendship, or make tomorrow’s 8 a.m. class disappear.

Unfortunately.

What it can do is give you a small moment of pleasure when the rest of the day hasn’t been particularly generous.

And maybe that’s enough.

We sometimes talk about food as if every meal needs to be perfectly nutritious, perfectly balanced, and carefully planned. Of course, nutrition matters. But food is also social. It is cultural. It is emotional. We celebrate with it, connect over it, and sometimes use it to take care of ourselves.

The problem begins when food becomes the only way we know how to cope.

There is a difference between “I’m having a horrible day, so I’m making myself some hot chocolate” and “I’m having a horrible day, and eating is the only thing that makes me feel okay.”

The first can simply be comfort.

The second might be a sign that something else needs attention.

So maybe comfort food doesn’t need to be either glorified or villainised. It can just be what it is: a small form of comfort.

Sometimes that means making a bowl of tomato soup and grilled cheese when you don’t feel like talking. Sometimes it’s ordering pizza with your friends after a terrible week. Sometimes it’s eating cereal straight from the bowl while watching an old movie you’ve seen seventeen times.

And sometimes it’s sitting on the kitchen floor at midnight, eating leftover pasta because the day was terrible and you needed something that didn’t ask questions.

Not every bad day needs a life lesson.

Sometimes you just need something warm, something familiar, and a little time.

The next morning, the problem might still be there. The deadline might still exist. The awkward conversation might still need to happen.

But for tonight, at least, you had dinner. And maybe that is a pretty good place to start. :)
