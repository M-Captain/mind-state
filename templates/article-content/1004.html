What if they find out?

The strange feeling of not believing you deserve the things you worked for.

You made it.

You got the grade you had been studying for for what felt like forever.

Everyone is proud of you. Your parents, your teachers, even your fellow students who were studying alongside you. They all think you did a good job.

And you did.

You studied hard. You worked for it. You reached the goal you had set for yourself.

So why is the only thought you can seem to hold onto:

“It was just luck.”

Maybe the exam was easy.

Maybe the teacher was generous.

Maybe everyone else simply did worse.

You push the thought away.

A few weeks later, the same thing happens again.

Someone compliments your art.

You laugh it off.

“It’s not that good.”

or

“Everyone else did way better.”

A month later, you get accepted into the university you desperately wanted to go to.

You stare at the email.

You should be thinking:

“I did it!”

Instead:

“Was the competition weaker this year?”

And there it is again.

That uncomfortable little voice telling you that somehow, somewhere along the way, you convinced everyone that you're more capable than you really are.

The fear that eventually, someone will notice.

That they'll look at you and think:

“Wait. You're not actually supposed to be here.”

That feeling has a name.

Imposter syndrome.

But I actually did it.

That's the weird thing about imposter syndrome.

You’re not necessarily bad at something. Mostly, it's almost the opposite.

You can have the grades, the awards, the compliments, the opportunities. You can have years of evidence that you're capable.

And still feel like you're pretending.

Your brain finds a reason for every success.

Good grade? Easy test.

Getting selected? Nobody else applied.

Someone complimented you? They're just being nice.

Success? Luck.

And somehow, your failures determine who you really are.

One bad grade? That’s just who I am.

One awkward presentation? Everyone can tell I'm terrible at this.

One mistake? I knew I didn't belong here.

Your successes become exceptions.

Your failures become evidence.

And before you know it, you've created a version of reality where almost nothing you do can convince you that you're actually good enough.

The finish line keeps moving

Maybe you think you'll finally feel confident once you achieve the thing.

Once you get the grade.

Once you win the competition.

Once someone finally tells you that you're good enough.

And then you get there.

But the finish line moves.

Right when you're about to reach it.

An A becomes an A+.

Being good becomes being the best.

Doing well becomes never struggling.

Eventually, you're not even chasing success anymore.

You're chasing the feeling that you're allowed to have succeeded.

The problem is that feeling never seems to arrive.

There's always another thing you could have done better. Someone you could be more like. Something you could achieve next.

So you keep going.

Not because you want to anymore, but because you're scared that if you stop, someone might finally notice you're not as capable as they thought.

Then you look around

And this is where it gets unfair.

Everyone else seems so sure of themselves.

You see someone get an A after saying they barely studied.

Your friend got the internship that rejected you.

You open Instagram and see someone your age winning competitions, travelling, creating things, pushing forward.

And suddenly you're wondering:

“What am I even doing?”

But there's something we forget when we compare ourselves to other people.

You're seeing the finished version.

You don't see their mistakes.

You don't see the things they don't understand.

You don't see the nights they felt completely lost.

You don't see them questioning themselves.

You only see what they decided to show you.

But here's what you don't see.

You're not the only one struggling with imposter syndrome.

It's actually quite common.

Many teens, and even adults, experience it.

When getting into university.

When joining a new work environment.

When stepping into any room where everyone else seems to know what they're doing.

You're not alone with that feeling.

And the strange thing is, it tends to hit the people who care the most.

The ones who take their work seriously.

Who want to get it right.

Who actually belong.

Because if you didn't care, you wouldn't be worrying whether you're good enough in the first place.

So… am I actually an imposter?

Probably not.

Feeling like an imposter doesn't mean you are one.

You don't have to know everything to deserve your place somewhere.

You don't have to be the smartest person in the room.

You don't even have to feel confident.

Sometimes you'll be the person who knows the least.

Sometimes you'll ask the question everyone else seemed to already know the answer to.

Sometimes you'll mess something up.

None of that means you tricked anyone.

It means you're learning.

And learning is supposed to feel uncomfortable sometimes.

Sometimes belonging looks like sitting in a room thinking,

“I have absolutely no idea what I'm doing.”

…and staying anyway.

Maybe we're asking the wrong question

We spend so much time asking:

“Am I good enough?”

But that's a horrible question to live by.

Because there will always be someone better. Another achievement. Another grade. Another person to compare yourself to.

So maybe you're allowed to still be learning.

To be nervous.

To not know.

To get things wrong.

Maybe you're even allowed to be proud of yourself without explaining to yourself why it doesn't count.

And maybe

just maybe

you didn't fool anyone.

Maybe the achievement wasn't a fluke.

Maybe you actually did earn your place.

And maybe the problem was never that you weren't good enough.

Maybe you just haven't learned how to believe it yet.
